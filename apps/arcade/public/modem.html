    WHAT YOU ARE HEARING

    A V.22 bis modem is two modems in one pair of wires. The calling end transmits on a 1200 Hz carrier and listens on 2400 Hz; the answering end does the opposite. Both are talking the whole time, which is why the sound is a mush of two things at once — and why the answering end adds an 1800 Hz guard tone six decibels down, to keep the telephone network's own signalling gear from mistaking the high channel for a control tone.

    Both channels run at 600 baud — six hundred symbols a second, always, at every speed. 1200 bit/s carries two bits per symbol as a phase change; 2400 bit/s carries four, the first two as the same phase change and the last two choosing one of four amplitudes inside the new quadrant. That is the whole difference between the two speeds, and it is why the handshake can decide between them so late.

    The tones you can pick out by ear fall straight out of that. Unscrambled binary 1 is an unbroken run of 11 dibits, and 11 means "turn 270°" — a quarter turn backwards every symbol, six hundred times a second, which is a tone 150 Hz below the carrier. So the answering modem's opening signal is a pure tone at 2250 Hz, and the calling modem's reply sits at 1050 Hz. Neither frequency appears anywhere in the specification; they are what the arithmetic does.

    The S1 signal — the one that means "I can do 2400" — is an alternating pattern of 00 and 11 dibits: a quarter turn forward, then a quarter turn back, three hundred times a second. The phase ends up where it started, so unlike the run of ones this one does not shift the carrier; it leaves it standing and puts sidebands 300 Hz either side of it — 900 and 1500 Hz in the low channel. That is the warble in the middle of the handshake, and the panel on the right measures it out of the signal rather than taking my word for it.

    After that both ends switch on their scramblers and everything turns to hiss. A scrambler is a shift register that divides the data by 1 ⊕ x⁻¹⁴ ⊕ x⁻¹⁷, whose job is to guarantee the line keeps changing state even when the data is a long run of the same bit — a receiver recovers its clock from those transitions and would drift without them. Send it all ones and it emits pseudo-random noise, which is exactly what the two modems do while they train. The specification even provides for the scrambler getting stuck: sixty-four consecutive ones at its output and it inverts the next bit going in.

The answering modem hears scrambled binary 1 where it was listening for the double dibit, and both ends settle for 1200 bit/s — the V.22 handshake, which is the same sequence with the middle taken out. That is the negotiation: one 100-millisecond pattern, present or absent.

    HOW HONEST IS THIS

    The signal is real: scrambler, differential quadrant encoding, the 16-point constellation, raised-cosine pulse shaping, both carriers summed on one line, guard tone at the specified 6 dB down, and the whole thing band-limited to the 300–3400 Hz a telephone circuit will pass and sampled at 8 kHz like one. Put a real demodulator on the output and the data is there to be read.

    The negotiation is staged rather than closed-loop: both ends are driven by the specification's timings instead of each actually detecting the other, because two real detectors would sound identical and take a week. The dial tone, DTMF and ringback are the North American precise tone plan; the ring cadence is shortened, which is the one duration on this page that is a lie.
